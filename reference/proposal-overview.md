# A Hierarchical Structure-Building Method for Foreign-Language Learning

## 1. Overview

This is a hypothetical method for teaching foreign languages by guiding learners from specific expressions to increasingly abstract grammatical patterns.

Its central hypothesis is that learners need to acquire language as a **hierarchical system**. A sentence is not merely a linear sequence of words. Words combine into phrases, phrases combine into larger phrases, and these structures combine into clauses.

For example:

> The student read the book on the desk.

has a hierarchical structure such as:

> [Finite clause
>  [NP The student]
>  [VP read
>   [NP the book
>    [PP on
>     [NP the desk]]]]]]

The phrase *the book on the desk* functions as a unit, while also containing the smaller phrase *on the desk*. A learner who sees only a sequence of words may have difficulty understanding which elements belong together, which expressions can be replaced as units, and how longer sentences are constructed.

The proposed method therefore gives learners repeated practice in building the intermediate structures of the target language. In general, learning proceeds through:

> specific expressions → partially open patterns → abstract constructions

Learners repeatedly connect forms with meanings, first through concrete examples and later through broader grammatical generalizations.

## 2. The problem with sentence-centred grammar teaching

Traditional grammar teaching often uses the complete sentence as its main unit.

Learners may study:

> The boy put the book on the table.

They may translate it, memorize it, or transform it into a negative sentence or question. However, they may receive little practice constructing the smaller units inside it:

> [NP the boy]
> [NP the book]
> [PP on [NP the table]]
> [VP put [NP the book] [PP on [NP the table]]]

Grammar is also commonly divided into separate topics such as articles, plurals, adjectives, prepositions, verb patterns, tense, negation, and questions. Although these categories are useful, learners must eventually integrate several of them within a single hierarchical structure.

A learner may know the rules for articles, adjective order, and plurals separately, yet still struggle to construct:

> [NP those two interesting books about language]

Likewise, a learner may know the words in a sentence without understanding how the verb organizes the participants and other elements around it.

The proposed method addresses this problem by making the construction of phrases and clauses a central learning activity rather than leaving their internal organization implicit.

## 3. Underlying assumptions

### 3.1 Language is hierarchically organized

Linguistic expressions contain units at several levels.

For example:

> house
> [NP the house]
> [NP the old house]
> [NP the old house [PP near the station]]
> [Finite clause I saw [NP the old house [PP near the station]]]

Previously learned structures can be inserted into larger ones. The learner therefore develops an understanding of both the parts and the relationships among them.

### 3.2 Learning generally moves from specific to abstract

Learners should not normally begin with the most abstract grammatical rule.

They may first learn:

> I want water.
> I want this one.
> I want to go home.

Through repetition and variation, they may develop the partially open pattern:

> I want ___.

At this stage, the learner can use the pattern productively without fully analysing it as a first-person subject, verb, and complement. Later, the learner may connect it with related patterns:

> You want ___.
> She wants ___.
> Do you want ___?
> I wanted ___.

More abstract grammatical knowledge can then emerge from a sufficiently rich set of familiar examples.

### 3.3 Form and meaning should be practised in both directions

Learners should repeatedly move between meaning and linguistic form.

They may receive a picture, animation, action, situation, or semantic representation and produce the corresponding expression. They may also hear or read an expression and identify or reconstruct its meaning.

For example, a learner may see a scene in which a girl places a cup on a table and produce:

> The girl is putting the cup on the table.

In the reverse direction, the learner may hear the sentence and arrange objects to represent it.

This ensures that grammatical practice remains connected to what the expression describes or accomplishes.

## 4. The first requirement: a grammar of the target language

To put the method into practice, curriculum designers must first build a practical grammar of the target language.

This grammar should describe its hierarchical structures, including:

* the main phrase and clause types;
* the elements that can occur inside them;
* the order or morphological marking of those elements;
* agreement, case, tense, and other formal requirements;
* the meanings associated with the patterns;
* restrictions, irregularities, and common extensions;
* how smaller structures are embedded within larger ones.

The grammar does not need to resolve every theoretical question about the language. It must be detailed enough to determine what learners should practise and in what sequence.

The general hypothesis may apply cross-linguistically, but each implementation must be language-specific. Languages differ in word order, case marking, agreement, morphology, argument structure, and the ways they organize information. The English sequence described below is therefore only one example.

## 5. The general learning cycle

At each stage, learners follow a similar process.

First, they encounter frequent expressions connected with clear meanings.

Next, they practise comprehension and oral production in both directions: from form to meaning and from meaning to form.

They then encounter controlled variation:

> Give me the book.
> Give me the cup.
> Give me that one.

This supports a partially open pattern:

> Give me ___.

The open position is gradually filled with new and increasingly complex material:

> Give me the red cup.
> Give me the book on the table.

Finally, the learner connects the pattern with related structures:

> Give her the book.
> He gave me the book.
> Did he give you the book?

Explicit explanations can be introduced when they help learners understand and organize patterns they have already experienced.

## 6. An English application

One possible English curriculum would begin with noun phrases, followed by prepositional phrases, verb phrases, finite clauses, and more complex structures.

### 6.1 Noun phrases

English learners, especially speakers of languages such as Japanese, need to recognize that there is a grammatical layer between a bare lexical noun and its use inside a verb phrase or prepositional phrase.

A noun such as *book* is not always inserted directly into a sentence. English frequently requires the construction of a larger noun phrase:

> book
> [NP a book]
> [NP the book]
> [NP two books]
> [NP an interesting book]
> [NP those two interesting books]

Japanese often allows nouns to occur without articles or obligatory number marking in contexts where English requires choices among forms such as *a book*, *the book*, and *books*. Japanese-speaking learners may therefore benefit from treating noun-phrase construction as a separate and fundamental skill.

Learners practise how determiners, number, adjectives, possessives, and modifiers combine around the lexical noun:

> [NP those two interesting books [PP about language]]

This establishes the noun phrase as a reusable unit that can later fill positions inside other structures.

### 6.2 Prepositional phrases

Learners then combine prepositions with complete noun phrases:

> [PP in [NP the room]]
> [PP under [NP the table]]
> [PP with [NP my friend]]

These structures can be inserted into larger phrases:

> [NP the book [PP on [NP the table]]]
> [VP sleep [PP on [NP the sofa]]]

This stage reinforces the fact that a preposition generally combines with a noun phrase rather than directly with a bare noun.

### 6.3 Verb phrases

Learners next construct verb phrases by filling the positions associated with different verbs and event patterns:

> [VP sleep]
> [VP read [NP the book]]
> [VP give [NP the student] [NP a book]]
> [VP put [NP the bag] [PP under [NP the chair]]]

Practice begins with specific expressions:

> read the book
> give me the key
> put the cup on the table

Learners then develop partially open patterns:

> read ___
> give me ___
> put ___ on ___

Previously learned noun phrases and prepositional phrases can now be inserted into these positions:

> [VP put
>  [NP the small red bag]
>  [PP under [NP the wooden chair]]]

### 6.4 Finite clauses

Once learners can construct verb phrases, they combine them with subjects, tense, aspect, modality, negation, and interrogation:

> [Finite clause She [VP reads the book]].
> [Finite clause She [VP read the book]].
> [Finite clause She is [VP reading the book]].
> [Finite clause She can [VP read the book]].
> [Finite clause She does not [VP read the book]].
> [Finite clause Does she [VP read the book]]?

Learners can see these as related structures built around a shared predicate, rather than as unrelated grammar points.

### 6.5 More complex structures

Familiar structures are gradually embedded within larger ones:

> [NP the book]
> [NP the book [PP on the table]]
> [NP the book [Relative clause that I bought]]
> [Finite clause I think [Complement clause that she bought the book]]

The curriculum continues by identifying and practising the major hierarchical structures of English.

## 7. Pronunciation and oral retrieval

Pronunciation should be established early and practised throughout the method.

For spoken languages, learners should ideally form an accurate sound representation before spelling becomes dominant. When learners begin with written forms, they may interpret them through the pronunciation and spelling conventions of languages they already know. Once an inaccurate pronunciation has become habitual, correcting it may be difficult.

A useful sequence is:

1. hear the expression;
2. connect it with its meaning;
3. imitate it;
4. retrieve it orally;
5. connect it with its written form.

Writing does not need to be postponed completely. The principle is that learners should not know an expression only as a written sequence.

Oral quizzes may also strengthen memory. Producing an answer aloud requires retrieval, articulation, and auditory feedback. Repeated oral production may help knowledge become faster and more procedural, although this explanation should be tested empirically.

Pronunciation practice should occur at every structural level:

> the old house
> near the station
> the old house near the station
> I saw the old house near the station.

This may also reinforce awareness of hierarchy because rhythm, stress, and pauses often correspond to phrase boundaries.

## 8. Potential limitations

The effectiveness of the method depends heavily on the quality of the language-specific grammar and curriculum.

Designers must determine:

* which structures should be introduced first;
* which specific expressions best support later generalization;
* how much variation learners need;
* when abstract explanations should be introduced;
* how irregular patterns and exceptions should be treated.

There is also a risk of excessive conscious analysis. Learners should not be required to construct a full syntactic representation whenever they speak. Hierarchical practice should eventually support automatic production.

The proposed English order may also work better for some learners than others. Its cross-linguistic validity and the importance of beginning with particular phrase types must be investigated separately for each language and learner group.

## 9. Summary

The proposed method teaches foreign languages by making their hierarchical grammatical structure visible and practicable.

Its implementation begins with a practical grammar of the target language. This grammar identifies the major phrase and clause types and explains how smaller units combine into larger ones.

Learners then proceed through:

> specific expressions → partially open patterns → abstract structures

In an English application, learners may begin with noun phrases because they need to recognize a layer between a bare lexical noun and its use inside prepositional phrases, verb phrases, and clauses. They then build prepositional phrases, verb phrases, finite clauses, and increasingly complex structures.

Throughout the process, learners connect meaning and form in both directions and practise expressions orally as well as in writing.

The central hypothesis is:

> Foreign-language learning may become more efficient when learners explicitly practise the hierarchical steps through which meaningful expressions are built, while developing abstract grammar gradually from specific and partially open patterns.
